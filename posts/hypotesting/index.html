The concept of hypothesis testing

  • A statistical hypothesis is a statement about the numerical value of a population parameter.
  • The null hypothesis, denoted by \(H_0\), represents the hypothesis that will be accepted unless the data provide convincing evidence that it is false. This usually represents the “status quo” or some claim about the population parameter that the researcher wants to test.
  • The alternative hypothesis, denoted by \(H_a\) represents the hypothesis that will be accepted only if the data provide convincing evidence of its truth. This usually represents the values of a population parameter for which the researcher wants to gather evidence to support.
  • The test statistic is a sample statistic, computed from information provided in the sample, that the researcher uses to decide between the null and alternative hypothesis.

One tailed vs. two tailed test

One-tailed test

A one-tailed test of hypothesis is one which the alternative hypothesis is directional and includes the symbol “\(\geq\)” or “\(\leq\)”.

Two-tailed test

A two tailed test of hypothesis is one in which the alternative does not specify departure from \(H_0\) in a particular direction and is written with the symbol of equality, i.e. “\(=\)”.

For example,

  • One tailed, upper tailed hypothesis \(H_a: \mu > 2400\).
  • One tailed, lower tailed hypothesis \(H_a: \mu < 2400\).
  • Two tailed hypothesis \(H_a: \mu \neq 2400.\)

Large sample test: \(z\) test

Example: Large sample one tailed test

Suppose building specifications in a certain city require that the average breaking strength of residential sewer pipe be more than 2400 kilograms per meter of length. Each manufacturer who wants to sell pipe in this city must demonstrate that its product meets the specification.

We want to check whether the mean breaking strength of the pipe exceeds 2400 kilograms per meter of length.

The null hypothesis would be,

\[ H_0: \mu \leq 2400 \]

i.e. the pipe does not meet the specifications.

And, the alternative hypothesis would be,

\[ H_1: \mu > 2400 \]

i.e. the pipe meets the specifications.

Convincing evidence in favor of the alternative hypothesis exist when the value of \(\bar{x}\) exceeds 2,400 by an amount that that cannot be attributed to sampling variability. To decide, we compute a test statistic called \(z\), a numerical value computed from the sample. It measures the distance between the value of \(\bar{x}\) and the value of \(\mu\) specified in the null hypothesis. Assume a \(95\%\) level of significance.

We can calculation the test statistic \(z\) like the following,

\[z = \frac{\bar{x}-2400}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}\]

Note that, a value of \(z=1\) means that the \(\bar{x}\) is \(1\sigma\) above \(\mu=2400\), a value of \(z=1.5\) means \(1.5\sigma\) above the \(\mu=2400\).

In our example,

\[ \alpha = \mathbb{P}(z>1.645)=0.05 \]

when, \(\mu=2400\). The shaded region in the histogram is called the rejection region that refers to the values of the test statistic for which we will reject the null hypothesis, i.e. reject \(H_0\) against \(H_1\).

Suppose we test 50 sections of sewer pipe and find the mean and standard deviation for these 50 measurements to be,

  • \(\bar{x}=2400\) kilo per linear meter of length.
  • \(s=200\) kilo per linear meter of length.

So, the test statistic will be,

\[ z = \frac{\bar{x}-2400}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}=\frac{2460-2400}{200/\sqrt{50}}=\frac{60}{28.28}=2.12 \]

Therefore, the sample mean lies \(2.12\sigma_{\bar{x}}\) above the hypothetical value of \(\mu\), 2400. Because this value of \(z\) exceeds 1.645, it falls in the rejection region. That is, we reject the null hypothesis that \(\mu=2400\) and conclude that \(\mu>2400\).

The sample mean lies \(2.12\sigma_{\bar{x}}\) above the hypothesized value of \(\mu\) and because this calculated value exceeds the table value 1.645, it falls in the rejection region. That is, we reject the null hypothesis.

Example: Large sample two tailed test

A manufacturer of cereal wants to test the performance of one of its filling machines. The machine is designed to discharge a mean amount of 12 kilo per box. The manufacturer wants to detect any departure from this setting. This quality study calls for random sampling 100 boxes and determining if the mean fill for the sample is 12 kilo per box.

The hypothesis would be,

  • \(H_0: \mu = 12\)
  • \(H_1: \mu \neq 12\)

Now assuming, \(\bar{x}=11.851\) and \(s=0.512\), \(\alpha=0.01\), we have,

\[ z = \frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\sigma_{\bar{x}}}=\frac{11.851-12}{0.512/\sqrt{100}}=\frac{-.149}{.512/10}=-2.91 \]

The decision rule for large sample two tailed \(z\) test is if the calculate value of \(z\) is greater than the table value of \(z\) we should reject the \(H_0\).

#Small sample test: \(t\)-test

Example: Small sample two tailed test

The owner of the local bar states that the average number of times he sees each of his customers during the month is 5. In order to validate his statement, he randomly selects 10 of his customers and determines the number of drinks each of them had during the previous year. He got the following values for 10 customers in his sample: 9, 10, 8, 4, 8, 3, 0, 10, 15, 9. Do the data supports his claim that the average number of times he sees a customer is 5?\

Null hypothesis

\[ H_0: \mu = 5 \]

The mean of the population the sample equals to 5.

Alternative hypothesis

\[ H_1: \mu \neq 5 \]

The mean of the population the sample is not equal to 5. This is a non-directional hypothesis and be evaluated by two-tailed test.

Example: Small sample one tailed test

\[ H_1: \mu > 5 \]

The mean of the population the sample represents is greater than 5.

\[ H_1: \mu < 5 \]

The mean of the population the sample represents is less than 5.

The directional hypotheses are to be evaluated by one-tailed test.

The test statistic for one sample t-test is,

\[ t= \frac{\bar{X}-\mu}{s_{\bar{X}}} \]

where, the mean of \(X\) is,

\[ \bar{X} = \frac{\Sigma X}{n} = \frac{76}{10} = 7.6 \]

and, the standard error of the population mean is,

\[ s_{\bar{X}}=\frac{\tilde{s}}{\sqrt{n}} \]

where, the standard deviation of the sample, which can be expressed as,

\[ \tilde{s}=\sqrt{\frac{\Sigma X^2 - \frac{(\Sigma X)^2}{n}}{n-1}}=\sqrt{\frac{740 - \frac{(76)^2}{10}}{9}}=4.25 \]

So, out t-statistic would be,

\[ t= \frac{7.6 - 5}{1.34} = 1.94 \]

This is the calculated value of t-statistic. To decide on the hypothesis, we need to compare it with the table value of t-statistic.

The hypothesis is also to be tested at a given level of significance and a degrees of freedom of \(n-1\).

Decision rule: One/Two tailed test

  • In case of non-directional hypothesis, the null hypothesis can be rejected if the calculated absolute value is equal to or greater than the two-tailed tabled value.
  • In case of directional hypothesis, where we are testing if the population mean is greater than the value in the null hypothesis, the the null hypothesis can be rejected if the calculated t value is positive and that is greater than the tabled one-tailed t value.
  • In case of directional hypothesis, where we are testing if the population mean is less than the value in the null hypothesis, the null hypothesis can be rejected if the calculated t value is negative and the absolute value of calculated t is equal or greater than the tabled one-tailed t value.

Type I and Type II error

The Type I error occurs if the researcher rejects the null hypothesis in favour of the alternative hypothesis when, in fact, \(H_0\) is true. The probability of committing a Type I error is denoted by \(\alpha\).

The Type II error occurs if the researcher accepts the null hypothesis when, in fact, \(H_0\) is false, The probability of committing a Type II error is denoted by \(\beta\)

Observed significance level: \(p\) value

The observed significance level, or \(p\)-value, for a specific statistical test is the probability (assuming \(H_0\) is true of observing a value of the test statistics that is at least as contradictory to the null hypothesis, and supporting of the alternative hypothesis, as the actual one computed from the sample data.

For example, the value of the test statistic computed for the sample of \(n=50\) sections of sewer pipe was \(z=2.12\). Because the test is one-tailed, our \(H_a:\mu > 2400\). The observed significance level \(p\)-value for this test is,

\[ $p$-value $= \mathbb{P}(z>2.12)$. \]

Or equivalently the area under the standard normal curve to the right of \(z=2.12\), which is here \(0.017 (=0.5-0.483)\)

Decision rule: \(p\) values

  • If the test is one-tailed, the \(p\) value is equal to the tail area beyond \(z\) in the same direction as the alternative hypothesis. Thus, if the alternative hypothesis is of the form \(>\), the \(p\)-value is the area to the right of, or above, the observed \(z\)-value. Conversely, if the alternative is of the form \(<\), the \(p\)-value is the area to the left of, or below, the observed \(z\)-value.
  • If the test is two tailed, the \(p\) value is equal to the twice the tail area beyond the observed \(z\)-value in the direction of \(z\), i.e. if \(z\) is positive, the \(p\) value is the twice the area to the right of, or above, the observed \(z\) value.

\(p\) value: two tailed case example

Let the two tailed hypothesis,

\[ H_0: \mu=12 \]

against the alternative hypothesis,

\[ H_1: \mu \neq 12 \]

The observed value of the test statistics is \(z=-2.91\), and any value of \(z\) less than \(-2.91\) or greater than \(2.91\) would be more contradictory to \(H_0\). Therefore, the observed significance level for the test is,

\[ p-value= \mathbb{P}(|z|>2.91) \]

Thus, we calculate the area below the observed \(z\)-value and double it, ie. \(0.0018 \times 2 = 0.0036\).

Comparing two means: independent sampling

Conditions required for valid large-sample inferences about \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\)

  • The two samples are randomly selected in an independent manner from the two target populations.
  • The sample sizes, \(n_1\) and \(n_2\) are both large (i.e. \(n \geq 30\) and \(n_2 \geq 30\). Due to the Central limit theorem, this condition guarantees that the sampling distribution of \((\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)\) will be approximately normal regardless of the shapes of the underlying probability distribution of the populations. Also, \(s_1^2\) and \(s_2^2\) will provide good approximations to \(\sigma_1^2\) and \(\sigma_2^2\) when the samples are both large.

Comparing two means: population sampling

** Properties of the sampling distribution of \(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2\)**

  • The mean of the sampling distribution \((\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)\) is \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\).
  • If the two samples are independent, the standard deviation of the sampling distribution is \[ \sigma_{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \]

** Large sample CI for \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\)**

\[ (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sigma_{(\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2)} = (\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2) \pm z_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \]

Large sample hypothesis for \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\)

Hypothesis for one-tailed test:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_0 : (\mu_1 - \mu_2) &= D_0 \\ H_1 : (\mu_1 - \mu_2) &< D_0 \\ H_1 : (\mu_1 - \mu_2) &> D_0 \end{aligned} \]

Hypothesis for two-tailed test:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_0 : (\mu_1 - \mu_2) &= D_0 \\ H_1 : (\mu_1 - \mu_2) &\neq D_0 \end{aligned} \]

where \(D_0\) is the hypothesized difference between the means. This difference is often hypothesized to be equal to zero.

Test statistic:

\[ z = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)-D_0}{\sigma_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2}} \]

where:

\[ \sigma_{\bar{x}_1-\bar{x}_2} = \sqrt{\frac{\sigma_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{\sigma_2^2}{n_2}} \approx \sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} \]

Rejection region:

  • If \(z < z_{\alpha}\), or \(z > z_{\alpha}\) when \(H_1 : (\mu_1 - \mu_2) > D_0\).
  • In the case of a two-tailed test, \(|z| > z_{\alpha/2}\).

Small sample hypothesis for \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\) when \(\sigma_1 = \sigma_2\)

Conditions required for valid small-sample inferences about \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\):

  • The two samples are randomly selected in an independent manner from the two target populations.
  • Both sampled populations have distributions that are approximately normal.
  • The population variances are equal, i.e., \(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2\).

Since we assume the two populations have equal variances (\(\sigma_1^2 = \sigma_2^2 = \sigma^2\)), it is reasonable to use the information contained in both samples to construct a pooled sample estimator of \(\sigma^2\) for use in CI and test statistics.

Thus, if \(s_1^2\) and \(s_2^2\) are the two sample variances (both estimating the variance \(\sigma^2\) common to both populations), the pooled estimator of \(\sigma^2\), denoted as \(s_p^2\), is:

\[ s_p^2 = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2+(n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{(n_1-1)+(n_2-1)} = \frac{(n_1 - 1)s_1^2+(n_2 - 1)s_2^2}{n_1+n_2-2} \]

Small sample CI for \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\) (Independent samples):

\[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{s_p^2 \left( \frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)} \]

where \(t_{\alpha/2}\) is based on \((n_1+n_2-2)\) degrees of freedom. The test statistic is:

\[ t = \frac{(\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2)-D_0}{\sqrt{s_p^2 \left( \frac{1}{n_1}+\frac{1}{n_2}\right)}} \]

Small sample hypothesis for \((\mu_1 - \mu_2)\) when \(\sigma_1 \neq \sigma_2\)

When the sample sizes are equal (\(n_1 = n_2\)):

  • Confidence interval:

\[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2 + s_2^2}{n}} \]

  • Test statistic:

\[ t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2 + s_2^2}{n}}} \]

where \(t\) is based on \(\nu = n_1 + n_2 - 2 = 2(n-1)\) degrees of freedom.

When the sample sizes are not equal (\(n_1 \neq n_2\)):

  • Confidence interval:

\[ (\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2) \pm t_{\alpha/2}\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}} \]

  • Test statistic:

\[ t = \frac{\bar{x}_1 - \bar{x}_2}{\sqrt{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}} \]

where \(t\) is based on \(\nu = \frac{\frac{s_1^2}{n_1}+\frac{s_2^2}{n_2}}{\frac{s_1^2/n_1}{n_1-1}+\frac{s_2^2/n_2}{n_2-1}}\) degrees of freedom.

Comparing two means: paired difference experiments

In the paired/matched sample case, the standard \(t\)-test is invalid because the assumption of independent samples is violated. To solve this problem, we create a column containing the difference between two samples \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). We then use the mean of the sample mean difference \(\bar{d}\) to draw our inference.

Hypotheses:

\[ \begin{aligned} H_0: \mu_d &= 0 \quad [\text{i.e., } (\mu_1 - \mu_2)=0] \\ H_1: \mu_d &\neq 0 \quad [\text{i.e., } (\mu_1 - \mu_2) \neq 0] \end{aligned} \]

Test statistic:

\[ t = \frac{\bar{d}-D}{s_d/\sqrt{n_d}} \]

where \(s_d\) is the sample standard deviation of the differences. The hypothesis is to be tested by using \((n_d - 1)\) degrees of freedom.

One way table

One-way table In the general case of \(K\) categories, suppose that the \(H_0\) specifies \(\mathbb{P}_1, \mathbb{P}_2, \ldots, \mathbb{P}_K\) for the probabilies that an observation fall into the categories. Assuming, the probabilies are mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive, the sum of probabilies must be 1.

Category 1 2 \(\ldots\) K Total
Observed number \(O_1\) \(O_2\) \(\ldots\) \(O_K\) n
Probability (under \(H_0\)) \(\mathbb{P}_1\) \(\mathbb{P}_2\) \(\ldots\) \(\mathbb{P}_K\) 1
Expected number (under \(H_0\)) \(E_1=n\mathbb{P}_1\) \(E_2=n\mathbb{P}_2\) \(\ldots\) \(E_K=n\mathbb{P}_K\) n

\[H_0: \mathbb{P}_1 = \mathbb{P}_{1,0}, \mathbb{P}_2 = \mathbb{P}_{2,0} = \ldots = \mathbb{P}_k = \mathbb{P}_{k,0}\] where, \(\mathbb{P}_{1,0}, \mathbb{P}_{2,0} = \ldots = \mathbb{P}_{k,0}\) represent the hypothesized values of the Multinomial probabilies. The \(H_1\) is: at least one of the multinomial probabilities does not equal its hypothesized value. Reject hypothesis if: \(\chi^2 > \chi^2_\alpha\)

One-way table A random sample of \(n\) observations, each of which can be classified into exactly one of \(K\) categories, is selected. Suppose the observed numbers in each category are \(O_1\), \(O_2\), \(\ldots\), \(O_K\). If a null hypothesis (\(H_0\)) specifies probabilities \(P_1, P_2, \ldots, P_K\) for an observation falling into each of these categories, the expected numbers in the categories, under \(H_0\), would be as follows:

\[E_i = n\mathbb{P}_i\] for \(i = 1, 2, \ldots, K\).

If the null hypothesis is true and the sample size is large enough that the expected values are at least 5, then the random variable associated with \[\chi^2 = \mathlarger{\sum}_{i = 1}^K \frac{(O_i-E_i)^2}{E_i}\] is known as chi-square random variable, and has, to a good approximation, a chi-square distribution with \((K-1)\) degress of freedom.

One-way table Suppose that the Hershey Company wants to determine if customers have a preference for any of the following four candy bars: A, Mr. Goodbar; B, Hershey’s Milk Chocolate; C, Hershey’s Special Dark Mildly Sweet Chocolate; or D, Krackel. From a random sample of 200 people, it was found that 43 preferred Mr. Goodbar; 53 preferred Hershey’s Milk Chocolate; 60 preferred Hershey’s Special Dark Mildly Sweet Chocolate, and the remainder preferred Krackel. Test the null hypothesis that customers have no preference for any of the four candy bars against the alternative hypothesis that customers have a preference for at least one of the candy bars.

Solution: The null hypothesis states that customers have no preference for any of the four candy bars (A, B, C, and D). That is, all four candy bars are equally preferred: \[H_0: P_A = P_B = P_C = P_D = 0.25\] Since the null hypothesis states that each candy bar is preferred by \(25\%\) of the customers, it follows that each of the expected values will be 50: \[E_i= nP_i = 200 \times 0.25 = 50\]

One-way table

Type \(O_i\) \(E_i\) \((O_i-E_i)\) \((O_i-E_i)^2\) \((O_i-E_i)^2/E_i\)
A 43 50 -7 49 0.98
B 53 50 3 9 0.18
C 60 50 10 100 2
D 44 50 -6 36 0.72

The chi-square test statistic is \(\chi^2 = \sum [(O_i-E_i)^2/E_i]= 3.88\). From \(\chi^2\)-table, assuming \(df = K - 1 = 3\), we find that the table value at \(5\%\) is 7.81473. That means, we fail to reject the null hypothesis and conclude that the data are not statistically significant to conclude that customers have a preference for at least one of these candy bars. We must be careful not to conclude that all four candy bars are equally preferred; we can state only that the evidence does not support a preference.

Two way contingency table

Contingency table Suppose that a sample is taken from a population and the members can be uniquely cross-classified according to a pair of characteristics, A and B. The hypothesis to be tested is of no association or dependence in the population between possession of characteristic A and possession of characteristic B. For example,

  • A travel agency may want to know if there is any relationship between a client’s gender and the method used to make an airline reservation.

  • An accounting firm may want to examine the relationship between the age of people and the type of income tax return filed by these individuals.

  • A marketing research company may test if a customer’s choice of cereal is in some way dependent on the color of the cereal box.

Contingency table Assume that there are \(r\) categories for \(A\) and \(c\) categories for \(B\), so a total of \(rc\) cross-classifications is possible. The number of sample observations belonging to both the \(i\)-th category of \(A\) and the \(j\)-th category of \(B\) will be denoted as \(O_{ij}\), where \(i=1,2, \ldots r\), and \(j= 1, 2, \ldots c\). The following table is called \(r \times c\) contingency table.

Characterstic B
Characterstic A 1 2 \(\ldots\) c Total
1 \(O_{11}\) \(O_{12}\) \(\ldots\) \(O_{1c}\) \(R_1\)
2 \(O_{22}\) \(O_{22}\) \(\ldots\) \(O_{2c}\) \(R_2\)
\(\vdots\) \(\vdots\) \(\vdots\) \(\ldots\) \(\vdots\) \(\vdots\)
\(r\) \(O_{r1}\) \(O_{r2}\) \(\ldots\) \(O_{rc}\) \(R_r\)
Total \(C_1\) \(C_2\) \(\ldots\) C_c \(n\)

\(\chi^2\): Contingency table It can be shown that under the \(H_0\), the random variable associated with \[\chi^2 = {\sum}_{j=1}^c {\sum}_{i=1}^r \frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}\] has, to a good approximation, a \(\chi^2\)-distribution with \((r-1)(c-1)\) degrees of freedom. The approximation works well if no more than \(20\%\) of the estimated expected numbers \(E_{ij}\) is less than 5. Sometimes adjacent classes can be combined in order to meet this assumption.

The double summation implies that the summation extends over all \(rc\) cells of the table. Clearly, the \(H_0\) of no association will be rejected for large absolute discrepancies between observed and expected numbers, i.e. for high values of the statistic.

Contingency table Suppose that a sample of \(n\) observations is cross-classified according to two characteristics in an \(r \times c\) contingency table. Denote by \(O_{ij}\) the number of observations in the cell that is in the \(i\)-th row and \(j\)-th column. If the null hypothesis is \(H_0\): No association exists between the two characteristics in the population, then the estimated expected number of observations in each cell under \(H_0\) is,

\[E_{ij} = \frac{R_i C_j}{n}\]

where \(R_i\) and \(C_j\) are the corresponding row and column totals. A test of association at a significance level a is based on the following decision rule: reject if-

\[{\sum}_{j=1}^c {\sum}_{i=1}^r \frac{(O_{ij}-E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}} > \chi^2_{(r-1)(c-1), \alpha}\]

Contingency table example Suppose a study was conducted to determine whether "safety" or "sporty" comes to a person’s mind when they see or hear of a particular type of automobile: BMW, Mercedes, or Lexus. Use a \(\chi^2\) test to evaluate whether the products mentioned differ in their associations and are, thus, perceived as dissimilar (which is most likely desired by the marketer).

Contingency table example The null hypothesis to be tested implies that, in the population, the three types of automobiles are perceived as similar; that is, there is no association between the automobile type and customers’ perception of the car as being known for being sporty or being known for its safety. To test the null hypothesis of no association, we again ask how many observations we would expect to find in each cross-classification if that hypothesis were true.

Automobile Sporty Safety Total
BMW 256, \(\frac{330 \times 363}{513}=233.5\) 74, \(\frac{330 \times 150}{513}=96.5\) 330
Mercedes 41, \(\frac{83 \times 363}{513}=58.7\) 42, \(\frac{83 \times 150}{513}=24.3\) 83

The test of the null hypothesis of no association is based on the magnitudes of the discrepancies between the observed numbers and those that would be expected if that hypothesis was true.

Contingency table example The calculated \(\chi^2\) statistics would be,

\[\chi^2 = \frac{(256-233.5)^2}{233.5} + \frac{(74-96.5)^2}{96.5} + \frac{(41-58.7)^2}{58.7} + \frac{(42-24.3)^2}{24.3} + \frac{(66-70.8)^2}{70.8} + \frac{(34-29.2)^2}{29.2} = 26.8\]

The degrees of freedom are \((r-1)(c-1)\). Here, there are \(r = 3\) rows and \(c = 2\) columns in the table, so the appropriate number of degrees of freedom is as follows:

\[(r-1)(c-1) = (3-1)(2-1) = 2\]

From the \(\chi^2\) table, we find \(\chi^2_{2, 0.05} = 5.99147\).

Therefore, the null hypothesis of no association is very clearly rejected at \(5\%\) level. The evidence against this hypothesis is overwhelming.

F-Test and Joint Hypotheses

When analyzing data across multiple groups or treatments, individual \(t\)-tests evaluate whether two specific group means differ. However, when we want to evaluate multiple conditions simultaneously, testing them pairwise inflates the risk of false findings.The F-test evaluates a joint hypothesis: it tests whether a set of two or more population parameters are all simultaneously equal to a baseline value.

Defining the Null and Alternative Hypotheses

To perform an \(F\)-test across \(k\) different population groups with true means \(\mu_1, \mu_2, \dots, \mu_k\), we formally state our hypotheses as follows:

Null Hypothesis: (\(H_0\))The null hypothesis states that all group means are equal. In other words, the treatment or group factor has no effect whatsoever:

\[H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2 = \dots = \mu_k\]

Key Concept: Notice the structural difference between \(t\)-tests and \(F\)-tests. A \(t\)-test tests a single equality (\(H_0: \mu_1 = \mu_2\)), whereas an \(F\)-test involves multiple equal signs simultaneously in \(H_0\).

Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\) or \(H_a\))

The alternative hypothesis is the logical negation of the null hypothesis. It does not state that all means are different from each other; it only requires that at least one equality fails:\[H_1: \text{At least one } \mu_i \neq \mu_j \quad \text{for } i \neq j\](Or simply: “Not all group means are equal.”)

Why Do We Need a Joint Hypothesis Test?

Suppose an agricultural study tests \(k = 4\) distinct soil treatments (Control, Treatment A, Treatment B, Treatment C) on crop yield:Null Hypothesis (\(H_0\)): \(\mu_{\text{Control}} = \mu_A = \mu_B = \mu_C\)Alternative Hypothesis (\(H_1\)): At least one soil treatment yields a different average crop output.

The Pitfall of Running Separate \(t\)-Tests

If you skip the joint \(F\)-test and instead run separate individual \(t\)-tests for every pairwise combination (6 distinct pairs for 4 groups) at significance level \(\alpha = 0.05\), the probability of committing at least one Type I Error (falsely rejecting a true null hypothesis) inflates rapidly:\[\text{P}(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1 - \alpha)^m\]Where \(m\) is the number of pairwise tests (\(m = \frac{k(k-1)}{2}\)). For 4 groups (\(m = 6\)), the false-positive risk climbs to:\[1 - (1 - 0.05)^6 \approx 26.5\%\]The \(F\)-test avoids this by evaluating all conditions in a single metric, maintaining the overall Type I error rate strictly at \(\alpha = 0.05\).

Intuition Behind the F-Statistic: Comparing Variances

The \(F\)-test evaluates \(H_0\) by partitioning the total variation into two components:

\[F = \frac{\text{Variation Between Groups}}{\text{Variation Within Groups}} = \frac{\text{Mean Square Between (MSB)}}{\text{Mean Square Within (MSW)}}\]

  • Variation Between Groups (Numerator): Measures how far sample group means (\(\bar{Y}_j\)) deviate from the overall grand mean (\(\bar{Y}\)). If \(H_0\) is true, this variation is purely due to random sampling noise.
  • Variation Within Groups (Denominator): Measures random scatter within each individual group around its own sample mean.When \(H_0\) is true, both MSB and MSW estimate the same error variance, yielding \(F \approx 1\). When \(H_0\) is false (at least one mean differs), MSB grows substantially larger than MSW, driving \(F\) significantly above \(1\).
